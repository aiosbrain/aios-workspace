Onboarding a carrier should take a fortnight, not a quarter.

A ten-week engagement to rebuild how a partner gets from signed to sending.

For Northwind Labs · D. Mensah · March 2027
The diagnosis

You do not have an integration problem. You have eleven handoffs and nobody who owns the wait.

The build takes four days. The other thirty-seven are queueing.

What we heard

Everyone is waiting for someone who is waiting

Fourteen conversations, one story, told to us in four different vocabularies.

  • "I send the mapping, then I chase." Partner success hands a field mapping to engineering and has no view of what happens to it next.
  • Nobody can name the approver. Legal, security and data each review. None of them owns the clock, so a two-hour read waits nine days.
  • The template is a screenshot. The onboarding steps live in a deck from 2025 that four people have privately forked.
  • Go-live is a surprise. Operations learns a partner is live when the first exception lands in the queue.
Fourteen interviews across engineering, partner success and operations, 2–20 February 2027. Quotes are composites. Everything on this slide is fictional.
Cause and consequence

One missing owner, four expensive symptoms

The cause is structural. The symptoms look like effort problems, which is why they keep getting answered with effort.

Cause
Nobody owns the clock
  • Eleven handoffs, each with a named doer and no named owner
  • Three reviewers, none of whom has a committed response window
  • No timestamp on any wait, so the delay is invisible until it is a quarter
  • Escalation is a favour asked in a corridor, not a route
Consequence
Effort is spent answering structure
  • Partner success spends roughly a day a week chasing
  • Engineering re-reads a mapping it already read, three weeks later
  • Four private forks of the onboarding checklist, none authoritative
  • The two partners with an internal champion go live in nine days; the rest do not

The recommended path

Ten weeks, four moves, an owner from week one

There is no discovery phase. The first real partner travels the new path in week five, while the old one still works.

  1. Week 1. Name the owner. One person owns the partner clock end to end, with standing authority to escalate past a silent reviewer.
  2. Weeks 1‑3. Instrument the wait. Every handoff gets a timestamp, so where the time goes stops being a matter of opinion.
  3. Weeks 3‑7. Rebuild the path. One sequence, one checklist, review windows agreed in advance instead of requested each time.
  4. Weeks 5‑10. Run it live. Three partners travel the new path, one of them deliberately awkward, while the old path stays open.
Weeks overlap by design. Ten calendar weeks, not ten sequential phases.

Before you say yes

What would make this not work

Three conditions. If any of them is not true in week one, better to say so now than in week six.

  • The owner has to be real. A named owner without authority to escalate past a silent reviewer is a coordinator, and coordinators are what you already have.
  • The old path stays open. We migrate nobody. Three partners travel the new path while the existing one still works for everyone else.
  • Review windows are committed, not requested. Legal, security and data each commit to a window in advance. Without that we have measured the wait rather than removed it.
Risk. If the pilot partners are picked for being easy, the new path passes and then fails on the next hard one. We pick one awkward partner on purpose, and we agree which one in week two.
Assumption. Timestamps come from your existing ticket and mail systems. Nothing new is purchased for this engagement.
